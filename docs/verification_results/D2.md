## How D2 was run

`scripts/run_d2.py`, all five seeds, following evaluation methods v1.3; the run took 381 min. Results are committed in `ec6692d`. For every test patient, each step's options were compared only on that fold's inner validation patients (averaged over seeds) and kept only if they helped. Each test patient was scored once, with its fold's chosen setup. The steps, in order:

1. per-patient normalization (on or off);
2. context of 0, 2, 5 or 10 min;
3. time-of-day features (on or off);
4. TUSZ interictal EEG in training (on or off);
5. gradient boosting or a small neural network;
6. alarm smoothing and persistence.

A one-seed preview under v1.2 is kept in `results/d2/preview_v1.2_seed0/`. It isn't the D2 result, but it led to the v1.3 changes, which are recorded in the evaluation methods' version history.

## VT-16 Alarm logic unit tests: PASS

`tests/test_alarm.py` checks four cases:

* a score that stays above threshold for 2 h gives alarms at least 30 min apart;
* a spike shorter than the persistence setting gives no alarm;
* a score bouncing around the threshold gives no repeat alarms inside the refractory period;
* changing scores after time t doesn't change any alarm before t, so the logic is causal.

The persistence rule also has its own test. All 168 tests pass.

## Patient-specific test (characterization)

This test was added in evaluation methods v1.3, after the D2 preview. It doesn't replace VT-11 or VT-15. It uses the D1 model and features unchanged, leaving one seizure out at a time within each patient, with the buffers described in Section 11 of the evaluation methods. A rerun gave identical results.

| | AUROC |
|---|---|
| **Patient-specific EEG model (22 patients)** | **0.662 (95% CI 0.588–0.742)** |
| Patient-specific clock-only model, same windows | 0.600 |
| Same 22 patients, cross-patient D1 | 0.568 |
| Same 22 patients, cross-patient D2 (includes time of day) | 0.616 |

The EEG model beat the patient's own clock-only model in 13 of the 22 patients tested.

### Findings

**F2-01. The cross-patient gain came from the time of day.** D2's improvement over D1 disappears without the time-of-day features: the brain-wave part scores 0.528, below D1's 0.556. D2 also scores below the clock-only model (0.614 vs 0.691). Adding EEG features that don't generalize across people dilutes the one signal that does.

**F2-02. No EEG change helped reliably across patients.** The time of day was chosen in all 25 folds. Normalization was chosen in 5, TUSZ in 3 and the neural network in 1. Context was chosen in 15 folds but didn't raise the brain-wave-only score. Choices made on about 5 inner validation patients per fold are noisy, and the scatter shows the differences between options are smaller than that noise.

**F2-03. Alarms are near chance, and thresholds don't transfer.** Sensitivity was 14–20% against a chance level of 10–12%, and the false alarm rate was above the target in every seed, even though thresholds met it on the inner validation patients.

**F2-04. EEG from another hospital looks unfamiliar to the model.** On 296 TUSZ patients the model never saw, it raised about 55 false alarms per 24 h, and a model can tell TUSZ recordings from CHB-MIT and Siena almost perfectly (0.959). A model trained on one hospital's equipment doesn't transfer to another's.

**F2-05. Personalization works better.** Trained on the same person's other seizures, the D1 model reaches 0.662 using EEG alone. That beats the cross-patient D2 model on the same patients (0.616) even though D2 uses the time of day, and it beats the patient's own clock-only model in 13 of 22 patients. The EEG does carry warning signs, but they differ between people. This supports a personalized design for AuraSense.

**F2-06. The mental arithmetic set is too short.** With 1.9 h, one alarm changes the rate by about 13 per 24 h, so it can't be interpreted (as noted in D1 finding F-03).

### Conclusion so far

Cross-patient prediction with this approach isn't acceptable as a warning system. Its AUROC of 0.614 comes almost entirely from the time of day, and its alarms are near chance. Personalized prediction from EEG alone is modest but real (0.662). The next planned experiment adds the time of day and the D2 improvements to the personalized model, and tests a general model adapted to each person, before the final D2 analysis.
