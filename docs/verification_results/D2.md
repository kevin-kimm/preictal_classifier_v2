## How D2 was run

`scripts/run_d2.py`, all five seeds, following evaluation methods v1.3; the run took 381 min. Results are committed in `ec6692d`. For every test patient, each step's options were compared only on that fold's inner validation patients (averaged over seeds) and kept only if they helped. Each test patient was scored once, with its fold's chosen setup. The steps, in order:

1. per-patient normalization (on or off);
2. context of 0, 2, 5 or 10 min;
3. time-of-day features (on or off);
4. TUSZ interictal EEG in training (on or off);
5. gradient boosting or a small neural network;
6. alarm smoothing and persistence.

A one-seed preview under v1.2 is kept in `results/d2/preview_v1.2_seed0/`. It isn't the D2 result, but it led to the v1.3 changes, which are recorded in the evaluation methods' version history.

## VT-16 Alarm logic unit tests: PASS

`tests/test_alarm.py` checks four cases:

* a score that stays above threshold for 2 h gives alarms at least 30 min apart;
* a spike shorter than the persistence setting gives no alarm;
* a score bouncing around the threshold gives no repeat alarms inside the refractory period;
* changing scores after time t doesn't change any alarm before t, so the logic is causal.

The persistence rule also has its own test. All 168 tests pass.

## Patient-specific test (characterization)

This test was added in evaluation methods v1.3, after the D2 preview. It doesn't replace VT-11 or VT-15. It uses the D1 model and features unchanged, leaving one seizure out at a time within each patient, with the buffers described in Section 11 of the evaluation methods. A rerun gave identical results.

| | AUROC |
|---|---|
| **Patient-specific EEG model (22 patients)** | **0.662 (95% CI 0.588–0.742)** |
| Patient-specific clock-only model, same windows | 0.600 |
| Same 22 patients, cross-patient D1 | 0.568 |
| Same 22 patients, cross-patient D2 (includes time of day) | 0.616 |

The EEG model beat the patient's own clock-only model in 13 of the 22 patients tested.

### Findings

**F2-01. The cross-patient gain came from the time of day.** D2's improvement over D1 disappears without the time-of-day features: the brain-wave part scores 0.528, below D1's 0.556. D2 also scores below the clock-only model (0.614 vs 0.691). Adding EEG features that don't generalize across people dilutes the one signal that does.

**F2-02. No EEG change helped reliably across patients.** The time of day was chosen in all 25 folds. Normalization was chosen in 5, TUSZ in 3 and the neural network in 1. Context was chosen in 15 folds but didn't raise the brain-wave-only score. Choices made on about 5 inner validation patients per fold are noisy, and the scatter shows the differences between options are smaller than that noise.

**F2-03. Alarms are near chance, and thresholds don't transfer.** Sensitivity was 14–20% against a chance level of 10–12%, and the false alarm rate was above the target in every seed, even though thresholds met it on the inner validation patients.

**F2-04. EEG from another hospital looks unfamiliar to the model.** On 296 TUSZ patients the model never saw, it raised about 55 false alarms per 24 h, and a model can tell TUSZ recordings from CHB-MIT and Siena almost perfectly (0.959). A model trained on one hospital's equipment doesn't transfer to another's.

**F2-05. Personalization works better.** Trained on the same person's other seizures, the D1 model reaches 0.662 using EEG alone. That beats the cross-patient D2 model on the same patients (0.616) even though D2 uses the time of day, and it beats the patient's own clock-only model in 13 of 22 patients. The EEG does carry warning signs, but they differ between people. This supports a personalized design for AuraSense.

**F2-06. The mental arithmetic set is too short.** With 1.9 h, one alarm changes the rate by about 13 per 24 h, so it can't be interpreted (as noted in D1 finding F-03).

## Personalized designs (characterization)

These tests use the 22 CHB-MIT and Siena patients with at least two eligible seizures and 1 h of interictal EEG. They leave one seizure out at a time within each patient (evaluation methods, Section 11), and every variant is scored on the same test windows. They were added and planned in writing before each run: v1.4 to v1.12 of the evaluation methods. "General" means a model trained on other patients only; "general + personal" adds this patient's own training windows, carrying half of each class's weight.

### Findings (continued)

**F2-07. Personalization is what makes EEG predictive.** Without the time of day, a model trained only on other patients is at chance (AUROC 0.529). Adding the patient's own data raises it to 0.681, improving 19 of 22 patients (p = 0.0015). The warning signs in scalp EEG appear to be real but specific to each person.

**F2-08. Richer features and a longer look-back didn't help.** Feature set v2 (spectral entropy, rhythm ratios and connectivity) matched v1 (0.696 vs 0.698, p = 0.98). A 30 min context hurt (0.654). The personal baseline helped slightly with personal data (0.709) but hurt the general model (0.614).

**F2-09. A correction to an earlier reading of the alarm results.** In the v1.5 alarm test, the general model beat chance once its threshold was set from the patient's own normal EEG (26% warned at 6.87 false alarms per 24 h). It was read at the time as showing that personal threshold tuning alone makes a general model useful. The frozen-design test shows that this relied on the time-of-day features. Without them, the general model stays at or below chance even with a personal threshold (4% warned at 4.76 per 24 h, chance 9%). For brain waves alone, the patient's own seizure data is what makes alarms beat chance.

**F2-10. The frozen design's alarms beat chance without the time of day.** At a target of 5 false alarms per 24 h it warned 16% of seizures (3.43 per 24 h; chance 7%, p = 0.003). At a target of 1 it warned 9% (1.41 per 24 h; chance 3%, p = 0.003). That is about 2–3 times chance. It is well short of a useful warning device, and far below the D2 target (80% at ≤ 5), but it is a real, time-of-day-free signal. The ≤ 1 target was slightly exceeded, as thresholds drift between the training and test periods.

**F2-11. The bottleneck is seizures per person, not normal EEG.** Training with a 1 h gap added 254 h of normal EEG without improving accuracy (0.684 vs 0.681). With 2–10 seizures per patient, the personal part has little to learn from.

**F2-12. The lead-up is dominated by changes in state and by artifacts.** In the plotted examples, the EEG 60, 20 and 5 min before a seizure looks similar. The largest changes in the lead-up look like changes in state (drowsiness, settling, waking), eye movements, movement and electrical interference, not seizure-specific signs. Seizures in children (chb01, chb05) are unmistakable; an adult focal seizure (Siena PN05) is subtle on scalp EEG. Amplitude differs about threefold between patients (channel spacing 230, 440 and 700 µV). These are engineering readings of three examples, not clinical interpretations.

**F2-13. Erratum: 2 of 8 homologous pairs never matched.** In feature set v2, the frontopolar pairs are spelled "FP1"/"FP2" while the derivations are named "Fp1"/"Fp2", so the left-right synchrony feature used 6 pairs. This was found after the freeze and kept unchanged, so that development and lockbox runs use identical code (evaluation methods v1.14; pinned by a test).

**F2-14. SeizeIT2's clock times are anonymized.** Every recording starts at midnight, in the EDF headers and in the annotation files. The dataset paper says most recordings are sequential within 24 h sessions. The frozen rules therefore place files back to back in run order (true distances can only be longer) and take preictal windows only from the seizure's own file (evaluation methods v1.13).

### Conclusion so far

Cross-patient prediction with this approach isn't acceptable as a warning system. D2's AUROC of 0.614 comes almost entirely from the time of day, and its alarms are near chance. Personalized prediction from brain waves alone is modest but real: AUROC about 0.68–0.70 on development data, and alarms 2–3 times better than chance, at about 1 in 6 seizures warned with 3.4 false alarms per 24 h.

The frozen design will be tested once on the 100 sealed SeizeIT2 patients. Before that come a planned 1 h sensitivity analysis and the SeizeIT2 adapter, built on the 25 development patients.
